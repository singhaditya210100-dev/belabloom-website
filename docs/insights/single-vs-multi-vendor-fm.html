Single-Vendor vs Multi-Vendor Facility Management: The Total Cost of Ownership

How integrated and multi-vendor facility management compare on total cost of ownership, including the hidden costs of coordination, procurement and compliance, and when each model fits.

Facility Management · 4 min read · By the Belabloom team · 24 October 2026

When companies compare facility management models, they usually compare contract prices. That misses most of the picture. The real comparison is total cost of ownership: what you pay vendors, plus what it costs you to manage them, plus the cost of things going wrong between them.

The three models

  • Multi-vendor: a separate contract for each service, such as housekeeping, security, pantry, electrical, air-conditioning, plumbing, pest control and fire maintenance. Your own team manages all of them.
  • Bundled: a few vendors, each covering a group of services, for example one for soft services and one for technical maintenance.
  • Integrated facility management (IFM): one partner manages all services under one contract, one set of service levels and one report, delivering some services directly and subcontracting specialists.

What goes into total cost of ownership

Cost elementMulti-vendorIntegrated
Contract pricesEach vendor adds its own margin and overheadsOne management fee; delivery costs can be pooled
Internal management timeHigh: your team coordinates every vendor dailyLower: one point of contact and one escalation path
Procurement and renewalsMany tenders, contracts and renewals each yearOne contract to tender, negotiate and renew
Coordination failuresIssues fall between vendors, like a leaking air-conditioning drain that is "a plumbing problem"One party owns the outcome
Compliance oversightStatutory records spread across many vendorsOne set of records and one compliance calendar
ReportingData in different formats, hard to consolidateOne dashboard across services and sites
Specialist depthYou can choose the best specialist for each serviceDepends on the partner's specialists and subcontractors
DependenceSpread across many vendorsConcentrated in one partner, so exit terms matter

A worked example

Take a hypothetical office where eight separate contracts add up to Rs 1 crore a year. The figures below are illustrative, not benchmarks; use your own numbers.

  • Internal management: two facility executives spend most of their time coordinating vendors. If their combined cost is Rs 18 lakh a year, and an integrated model frees up half of that time for other work, that is worth around Rs 9 lakh.
  • Procurement: eight tenders and renewals a year take weeks of administration, legal and finance time. One contract takes a fraction of that.
  • Downtime: if coordination failures cost a few hours of disruption a month in a large office, the productivity loss can easily exceed the price difference between models.

Even if the integrated contract price came in slightly higher than the sum of the separate contracts, the total cost could still be lower. Equally, if your internal team is strong and your vendors perform well, the multi-vendor model may already be the cheaper option. The point is to count every cost, not just the invoices.

When multi-vendor makes sense

  • A single small site with only a few services
  • A strong internal facility team that already manages vendors well
  • Highly specialised services where the best provider matters more than coordination

When integrated makes sense

  • Multiple sites or branches that need consistent standards and consolidated reporting
  • A lean internal team that should focus on the business, not vendor management
  • Growth plans, where opening new sites should not mean new contracts every time
  • Heavy compliance requirements, as in BFSI, healthcare or data centres

The hybrid that often works best

Many organisations integrate most services under one partner but keep certain specialist contracts direct, most commonly lift maintenance with the manufacturer and major equipment such as chillers and generators with their manufacturers or authorised service providers. The integrated partner coordinates these contracts and includes them in reporting, so you still get one view of everything.

How to evaluate the switch

  1. Build a baseline of all current contracts, internal time and known service failures.
  2. Define the service levels you want, then ask partners to price against them.
  3. Check the partner's statutory compliance and its process for managing subcontractors.
  4. Plan the transition: staff transfer, asset handover and a mobilisation period.
  5. Protect yourself with clear exit terms, and keep ownership of asset registers and maintenance records.
